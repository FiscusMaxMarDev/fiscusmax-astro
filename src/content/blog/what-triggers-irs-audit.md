Audits are rare, but the fear of one shapes how a lot of people file — sometimes causing them to under-claim legitimate deductions just to stay under the radar. Understanding what actually triggers scrutiny replaces vague anxiety with something far more useful: a clear sense of what to get right, and permission to claim everything you're actually entitled to.

## Mismatches Are the Most Common Trigger

The IRS receives copies of most of the same forms you do — W-2s, 1099s, K-1s — and its systems automatically compare what was reported to what you claim on your return. A mismatch, even an innocent one caused by a missed form or a typo, is one of the most reliable ways to generate an automated notice. This isn't usually a full audit in the dramatic sense; it's often a computer-generated letter asking you to explain or correct a discrepancy. Still, it's entirely avoidable simply by cross-checking every income form against what actually gets reported on the return before filing.

## Disproportionate Deductions Draw Attention

Deductions that look unusually large relative to reported income — a home office deduction that seems oversized for the space, business expenses that consume most of the revenue, charitable giving well above what similar income levels typically report — can draw a closer look. This doesn't mean legitimate large deductions should be avoided; it means they should be well-documented, because the IRS's systems are comparing your return to statistical norms, not reading your personal explanation before flagging it.

## Cash-Heavy and Self-Employment Income

Businesses that deal heavily in cash, and self-employed individuals generally, face somewhat higher scrutiny than W-2 employees, simply because there's more room for underreporting and less third-party verification of the numbers. That's not a reason to avoid self-employment or cash transactions — it's a reason to keep especially clean records, since the burden of proof in a review falls on demonstrating that reported numbers are accurate.

## The Best Defense Is Documentation, Not Caution

None of this means playing it safe by underclaiming what you're owed. The actual defense against an audit is accurate, well-documented reporting — not avoiding legitimate deductions out of fear. A well-documented, larger deduction survives scrutiny far better than a timid, underclaimed one that still gets flagged anyway.

> Could one overlooked document delay your refund or trigger a notice? Most audit triggers are avoidable with basic diligence, not by claiming less than you're entitled to.
